Who moved?

Millions of African Americans left the rural South for cities in other parts of the country. Many came from agricultural communities and worked as sharecroppers, tenant farmers, or laborers on plantations.

People moved as individuals and families, often following relatives and friends who had already settled in a destination city.

When?

The Great Migration took place from about 1910 to 1970. It happened in waves rather than all at once, with migration increasing during both World War I and World War II.

Wartime industries needed workers, creating new opportunities in cities. During World War II, Los Angeles attracted migrants with jobs in naval, shipbuilding, aircraft, and other military-related industries.

Where?

People moved from Southern states to cities across the country, especially in the North and Midwest. Popular destinations included:

  • Chicago, Philadelphia, Boston, and New York City
  • Washington, D.C., Milwaukee, and Los Angeles
  • Oklahoma City

Many migrants chose a city where they already had family, friends, or community connections. During World War II, Los Angeles became an important destination for people seeking work in naval and military industries.

Why?

Push factors

  • Jim Crow laws enforced segregation and denied basic rights.
  • Racial violence, threats, and discrimination made life unsafe and restricted opportunity.
  • The boll weevil damaged cotton crops, adding to hardship in Southern farming communities.

Pull factors

  • Industrial jobs and the chance to earn better wages
  • Hope for greater freedom and a new life
  • Demand for workers during World War I and World War II, including naval and military industries in Los Angeles
  • The Chicago Defender, which reported on life and opportunities outside the South

Conditions varied by city, and moving North did not end racism or discrimination. Still, many people saw migration as a chance to seek safety, opportunity, and more control over their lives.

The journey

Many migrants traveled north by railroad, carrying what they could and leaving home for a new city. Depending on the route and delays, a trip could take about one to two weeks.

Rail travel conditions varied. Some trains offered Pullman sleeping cars and more comfortable accommodations, while less expensive cars could be more crowded and uncomfortable.

Travelers also faced prejudice and discrimination, including being denied service or treated unfairly by railroad staff. Even reaching a new city could mean enduring uncomfortable conditions and unequal treatment along the way.

Letters, newspapers, and word of mouth helped families choose where to go. The Chicago Defender circulated widely and shared reports about jobs and Black life in cities beyond the South.

The journey could be uncertain: migrants had to find transportation, work, and a place to live, sometimes with help from relatives or people from their hometown.

Life after moving

Many migrants found work in factories, railroads, service jobs, and other industries. Factory work could require learning unfamiliar tasks and machinery. Jobs were often insecure: some employers could fire workers who became injured or could no longer keep up, and workers had few protections.

Affordable housing was scarce, so many families lived in crowded apartments or tenements and sometimes sold belongings to help pay rent. Pullman, near Chicago, was a company town built around the railroad industry. Black Pullman porters worked on the trains and helped serve passengers; the Brotherhood of Sleeping Car Porters, founded in 1925, organized for better pay and working conditions.

Compared with the Jim Crow South, many Northern cities offered greater political opportunities and fewer laws enforcing segregation in everyday life. But migrants did not find equality: prejudice and discrimination persisted in housing, jobs, schools, and public spaces.

Housing discrimination included redlining, a practice that denied or limited mortgages and investment in neighborhoods where Black residents lived. Racial hostility sometimes erupted into violence. During Chicago's 1919 race riot, white mobs attacked Black residents and neighborhoods; the violence was part of the wider Red Summer of 1919.

Despite these barriers, migrants built neighborhoods, churches, businesses, newspapers, and civic organizations. They helped transform city life and cultural movements such as the Harlem Renaissance, bringing Southern traditions, music, food, and community life to their new homes.
